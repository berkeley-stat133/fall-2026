Spatial Data I

Coordinate systems, datums, and projections

Last Time

  1. A duration is a fixed span in seconds.
  2. A period is a span in human units.
  3. UTC is the world’s reference clock.
  4. A time zone is an offset from UTC.

Key Ideas for Today

  1. A coordinate system locates points from an origin.
  2. A datum models the shape of the earth.
  3. A projection flattens the globe and distorts it.
  4. A simple feature pairs a geometry with attributes.

Concept Acquisition

  1. Spatial data are different: nearby observations tend to be similar, distances aren’t straight lines, coordinates come in pairs, and observations are often areas rather than points.
  2. A location is defined relative to an origin, in Cartesian or polar coordinates; longitude and latitude are polar coordinates on the earth.
  3. A datum is a model of the earth’s shape plus reference points; WGS 84 is the most common, and mapping data recorded in one datum onto another can put locations hundreds of meters off.
  4. A projection transforms ellipsoidal coordinates into projected coordinates and must distort at least one of area, shape, distance, or angles.
  5. A coordinate reference system (CRS) is a datum, a coordinate system, and optionally a projection; each has an EPSG code.
  6. A simple feature is an object with a geometry (point, point set, polygon, …) and non-spatial attributes.
  7. An sf object records its CRS; a data frame of longitude and latitude does not, and ggplot2 assumes it matches the map. Every layer needs a declared CRS before the layers can be brought into one.

Tool Acquisition

  1. geom_path() with group = to draw tracks from longitude and latitude columns.
  2. ne_countries() from rnaturalearth, with returnclass = "sf".
  3. st_crs() from sf to see an object’s coordinate reference system.
  4. geom_sf() to draw an sf object.
  5. st_as_sf() with coords = and crs = to turn a data frame of longitude and latitude into an sf object with a declared CRS.
  6. st_transform() to change an sf object’s CRS.

Concept Application

  1. Decide how to calculate the distance between the first two rows of storms.
  2. Layer the 1975 storms on a map of North America with geom_point() over geom_sf().
  3. See the storms vanish on an Albers map, then fix it with st_as_sf() and st_transform().

Characteristics of spatial data

  1. Nearby observations tend to be similar.
  2. Distances between observations are often not a straight line.
  3. Coordinate pairs must be treated as pairs, not individual variables.
  4. Observations are often recorded at the level of an area in space, not a point in space.

Your Turn

Question: Storm Amy moved from (−79, 27.5) to (−79, 28.5). The earth’s radius is about 6,371 km. How far did it travel?

02:00

Debrief on the board: 2π · 6371 / 360 ≈ 111 km per degree of latitude. Then ask: what if it had moved 1° east instead? Only 111 · cos(27.5°) ≈ 99 km, since longitude lines converge. This is why distance isn’t a straight line in (long, lat).

Relative to what?

Like the Unix Epoch, we need to store our measurements relative to an agreed upon reference point. Starting in 1901, that reference point for North America was Meades Ranch in Kansas. This system was formalized as the North American Datum (NAD 1927).

Datum

A particular mathematical model of the shape of the earth, along with reference points.

Most common datum for global data: WGS 84

What a difference a datum makes

Locations recorded in one datum and mapped in another can be 100s of meters off.

Guess the Metaphor

A datum is like a lighthouse: sailors fix every position by its bearing and distance from one agreed landmark. Take your bearings from a different lighthouse and every position on the chart shifts, sometimes by hundreds of meters.

Projections

Problem

How do you transform 3D coordinates on an ellipsoid into 2D coordinates in the plane?

You cannot without distorting at least one of:

  • area
  • shape
  • distance
  • angles

Projection

A function that transforms between ellipsoidal coordinates and projected coordinates.

  1. Ellipsoidal coordinates: (long, lat) locations on a shape approximating the Earth’s shape.

  2. Projected coordinates: coordinates on a flat, two-dimensional coordinate system, used when plotting maps.

Your Turn

Question: In which projection is Greenland about the size of Africa? Which is closest to the truth?

01:00

Ask: in which projection does Greenland look about as big as Africa? Africa is really about 14 times larger. Circle Greenland and Africa in Mercator and Gall-Peters. Mercator keeps shapes (angles) and pays in area; Gall-Peters, Goode, and Albers keep area and pay in shape. PS 5 uses Albers.

Coordinate Reference System (CRS)

Everything needed to say where on Earth a pair of coordinates is.

  1. Datum: the model of the earth (WGS 84, NAD83).
  2. Coordinate system: the axes, their order, and units (degrees, meters).
  3. Projection (optional): how the globe is flattened (Albers).

Each CRS has an EPSG code:

  • 4326: WGS 84, (long, lat) in degrees
  • 5070: NAD83 + Albers for the US, in meters
  • 3310: NAD83 + Albers for California, in meters

This ties the last two sections together: a datum alone doesn’t say what (−79, 27.5) means. You also need the coordinate system (degrees, lon first) and, for a flat map, a projection. 4326 has no projection; 5070 and 3310 are NAD83 plus an Albers projection, in meters. The storms case study uses 4326 and 5070; PS 5 uses 3310. Students will see all three parts when we run st_crs() later today.

  • storms is a data frame: two columns of numbers, no CRS.
  • Its datum is in the documentation (?storms: NOAA’s HURDAT), not the data.
  • ggplot() drew long as x and lat as y, stretched to fit the figure.

Fill in the storms row on the board: long/lat in degrees, datum not recorded, no projection. The aspect ratio of these plots came from the 14 × 9 figure, not from the earth.

Why the datum barely matters here: every lat and long in storms is rounded to 0.1°, about 11 km, while switching datums moves points by hundreds of meters. The projection matters a lot more for storms. A datum matters when the data are finer than the shift: GPS tracks, surveying, property lines (the datum gif earlier).

Simple Features

Feature

A “thing” or object that has a spatial location or extent; it may be a physical object like a building, or a social convention like a political state.

Feature geometry: the spatial properties (location or extent) of a feature, and can be described by a point, a point set, a polygon, etc.

Feature attributes: other non-spatial properties measured on the feature (e.g. color, name, landtype)

What happens if we want a new map projection?

$units_gdal pulls one piece out of a CRS, a shortcut for reading the long st_crs() printout. The storms’ numbers are degrees only because NOAA recorded them that way: storms75 is a data frame, so its CRS is NA and so are its units. ggplot2 drew those numbers in whatever units the map uses. Here that’s degrees; at the end of the case study it won’t be.

na_albers <- st_transform(map_north_america, crs = 5070)

ggplot() +
  geom_sf(data = na_albers) +
  theme(panel.background = element_blank())

Same two layers as the “Why did that work?” slide, but now the map is in meters. The storms layer didn’t change; only the map did, and ggplot2 still drew the storms in the map’s units. Fill in the na_albers row on the board if it isn’t there yet.

  • st_as_sf() declares the CRS the storms’ numbers are in.
  • st_transform() transforms them into the map’s CRS.
storms_albers <- storms75 |>
  st_as_sf(coords = c("long", "lat"), crs = 4326) |>
  st_transform(crs = 5070)

ggplot() +
  geom_sf(data = na_albers) +
  geom_sf(data = storms_albers, aes(color = name), size = 1) +
  theme(panel.background = element_blank())

Line 2 turns each row into a POINT and records that its numbers are WGS 84 degrees; line 3 converts them to Albers meters, the same function that made na_albers. Now both layers are sf objects in 5070, so geom_sf() draws both. PS 5 does exactly this with earthquakes in California.

Two asides if asked. geom_sf() will quietly reproject any sf layer into the first layer’s CRS, so the map still looks right if you skip line 3; line 2 is the step you can’t skip. And the storms are points now, not tracks: joining them back into lines takes summarize() on an sf object, which comes in Spatial II.

The rule

Every layer needs a declared CRS.

  • An sf object carries its own: check it with st_crs().
  • A data frame doesn’t: declare it with st_as_sf(coords = , crs = ).

If the layers have differing CRS, transform them with st_transform().

This answers “how do you move data from one datum to another?”: declare the CRS the data are actually in, then st_transform() to the one you want. Setting a new CRS on old numbers is how points end up hundreds of meters off, as in the datum gif.

Key Ideas for Today

  1. A coordinate system locates points from an origin.
  2. A datum models the shape of the earth.
  3. A projection flattens the globe and distorts it.
  4. A simple feature pairs a geometry with attributes.
